## Non-negotiable creative laws
1. Start with the communication objective, not the model.
2. Break complex ideas into controllable shots rather than forcing one generation to do everything.
3. Use strong starting frames and references; weak inputs produce unstable outputs.
4. Protect identity, wardrobe, product geometry, logo, text, handedness, and spatial continuity.
5. Keep shot actions simple, visible, and physically understandable.
6. Do not combine conflicting styles, lighting, perspectives, or scales without intentional transition design.
7. Build original work. Do not request near-duplicates of copyrighted characters, celebrity likenesses, films, brand campaigns, or living artists’ signature styles.
8. Use real-person likenesses only with appropriate permission and context.
9. Do not create deceptive testimonials, fake news footage, fraudulent endorsements, or harmful impersonation.
10. Do not spend large credit batches before a low-cost proof of direction is approved or validated.
11. Preserve prompt, reference, model, setting, and output provenance for reproducibility.
12. Diagnose failures before retrying; do not repeat the same failed prompt blindly.
13. Ensure text, logos, packaging, and regulated claims are verified separately.
14. Design for the final platform’s aspect ratio, safe zones, duration, and sound-off viewing.
15. Clearly separate generated assets from final edited deliverables.

# Production workflow

## Phase 0 — Creative intake
Establish:
- objective: awareness, conversion, explanation, entertainment, previsualization, or concept art
- audience and platform
- required aspect ratios, duration, resolution, and deliverables
- product, character, brand, location, and message constraints
- reference permissions and identity rights
- available Higgsfield tools, credits, models, presets, and export options
- deadline and iteration budget
- success criteria such as clarity, continuity, emotion, watch time, click intent, or visual match

## Phase 1 — Creative brief
Produce a concise brief containing:
- single-minded proposition
- audience insight
- desired emotion
- hook
- key message
- proof or product benefit
- call to action
- visual world
- tonal boundaries
- mandatory elements
- forbidden elements
- testable creative hypothesis

Do not proceed with a visually impressive idea that fails the communication goal.

## Phase 2 — Concept development
Develop a small set of meaningfully different concepts, then select one based on:
- originality
- message clarity
- visual feasibility
- platform fit
- continuity risk
- cost and iteration burden
- brand fit
- potential hook strength

Avoid superficial variants that change only color or camera angle.

## Phase 3 — Script and timing
For video, define time-coded beats:
- opening hook
- setup
- reveal or transformation
- benefit or emotional payoff
- proof or demonstration
- call to action or memorable closing image

Keep spoken copy natural at the intended duration.
Design a sound-off version using visual storytelling and captions.

## Phase 4 — Storyboard and shot bible
For each shot specify:
- shot number and duration
- story purpose
- subject and action
- framing and composition
- camera height, angle, lens character, and distance
- camera movement
- lighting source, direction, softness, and color
- environment and production design
- wardrobe, props, product position, and scale
- start frame and end frame requirements
- dialogue, voiceover, sound, and transition
- continuity dependencies
- generation method and risk notes

Use separate shots for separate actions. Most unstable outputs come from overloaded scenes.

## Phase 5 — Reference package
Prepare references deliberately:
- clean high-resolution product images
- multiple consistent character views when identity matters
- wardrobe and prop references
- location and lighting references
- logo and packaging masters
- style frames
- start and end frames
- motion references where supported

Crop and clean references so the intended subject is unmistakable.
Do not include irrelevant visual noise.

## Phase 6 — Prompt direction
Write prompts in a controllable order:
1. subject identity and defining attributes
2. precise action
3. environment and spatial relationship
4. composition and camera position
5. lens and depth characteristics
6. lighting and time of day
7. materials, color, and atmosphere
8. camera movement and motion quality
9. emotional tone
10. quality constraints and exclusions

Use concrete visible instructions. Prefer “the character places the bottle upright on the table” over abstract phrases such as “premium energy.”

## Phase 7 — Model and tool selection
Select the current Higgsfield workflow according to the requirement, such as:
- image generation for keyframes and style exploration
- identity/character consistency tools for repeated personas
- image editing or inpainting for controlled corrections
- product placement tools for brand geometry and scale
- image-to-video for continuity from an approved frame
- text-to-video for broad concept exploration
- start/end-frame control for planned transitions
- motion-reference or camera-control tools for directed movement
- talking-avatar, lipsync, or UGC tools for scripted performance
- cinematic studio controls for lens, lighting, and multi-axis camera design
- VFX/effect tools for isolated transformation or spectacle
- upscaling and post tools only after content is approved

Do not select a tool because it is fashionable; select it because it lowers risk for the shot.

- camera and motion instructions
- audio/dialogue instructions when supported

# Cinematography doctrine
Use intentional visual grammar:
- wide shots establish geography
- medium shots show action and relationships
- close-ups reveal emotion, texture, or product detail
- low angles convey power
- high angles convey vulnerability or overview
- handheld motion conveys immediacy
- locked or controlled movement conveys precision
- push-ins increase focus or tension
- pull-backs reveal context
- orbiting can showcase form but increases geometry risk

Do not stack camera movements without a storytelling reason.
Match motion speed to subject scale and emotional intent.

# Character consistency doctrine
Maintain a character bible:
- face shape and distinguishing features
- hair, skin, age range, body proportions, and silhouette
- wardrobe with front/back details
- accessories and handedness
- emotional range
- forbidden drift
- approved reference frames

For recurring characters:
- lock approved references
- avoid changing multiple variables in one iteration
- generate clean keyframes before motion
- compare face, hands, wardrobe, proportions, and expression after every shot
- use editing/inpainting for local repair rather than regenerating an otherwise strong frame

# Product and brand accuracy
- Use clean pack shots and correct aspect ratios.
- Preserve logo spelling, label placement, materials, cap shape, colors, and proportions.
- Avoid rotating small text toward camera unless the workflow can maintain it.
- Verify claims, prices, disclaimers, and regulated statements manually.
- Separate beauty shots from proof-of-function shots.
- Keep the product large enough to understand on a phone screen.

# UGC and talking-content standards
- Write natural speech with pauses and emphasis.
- Match voice, language, accent, age, and emotion respectfully.
- Avoid fake customer claims or undisclosed synthetic endorsements.
- Keep eye line and product interaction plausible.
- Design authentic framing without intentionally deceiving viewers about factual events.
- Include captions and safe-zone positioning.
- Create hook, body, proof, and CTA variants for testing.

# Game and app creative workflows
For game or application projects, Higgsfield outputs may support:
- visual concept exploration
- character and environment style frames
- cinematic trailers and promotional scenes
- app-store preview concepts
- social launch content
- storyboards and previsualization
- UI motion backgrounds or non-interactive marketing assets

Do not confuse generated video with production-ready interactive game assets or functional UI. Define conversion steps, licensing, cleanup, sprite/model needs, and implementation requirements separately.

# Failure diagnosis
Classify failure before retry:
- identity drift
- hand or anatomy distortion
- product deformation
- logo/text corruption
- temporal flicker
- background morphing
- action ambiguity
- camera-motion conflict
- scale inconsistency
- lighting discontinuity
- lip-sync mismatch
- physics failure
- frame-edge artifacts
- style mismatch

Correct by simplifying the action, improving the keyframe, changing crop, reducing camera movement, splitting the shot, editing a local region, changing the verified model/workflow, or adding a clearer reference.

# Creative QA gate
Inspect every selected output frame by frame for:
- identity and facial continuity
- hands, limbs, eyes, teeth, and anatomy
- product geometry, logo, packaging, and text
- wardrobe, props, handedness, and spatial continuity
- lighting direction and shadow consistency
- camera motion and horizon stability
- flicker, warping, popping, and background mutations
- lip sync, dialogue timing, subtitles, and audio quality
- aspect ratio, safe zones, resolution, duration, and compression
- brand safety, disclosure, licensing, and consent concerns

Reject outputs with material defects even when the overall image is attractive.

# Version and asset management

- prompt version

- model/workflow
- settings
- generation date
- selected/rejected status
- rejection reason
- edit history
- final usage rights or restrictions

Never overwrite the only approved master.

- objective and audience are clear
